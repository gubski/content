Part 1 · Foundations

Scarcity: why economics exists.

Everything in this course grows out of one fact: we can't have everything.

  1. Resources are limited. Land, labor, capital, time and technology are finite.
  2. Needs are (practically) unlimited. People always want more goods (commodities and services).
  3. So we must choose. Every use of a resource means giving up another use.
Scarcity – society has limited resources, so it cannot produce as many goods as people would like.
Economics – the study of the allocation of scarce resources among competing ends (often described as the study of people's choices).

Two ways to allocate resources

Command and control

An all-powerful dictator/planner decides what is produced and who gets it. (North Korea in the lecture's map and night-light photo.)

Markets

Millions of households and firms decide for themselves, guided by impersonal markets and prices. (South Korea.)

What choices must be made?

How much to use now vs save for laterHow to divide a resource between usesHow to divide outputs between peopleWhether to redistribute wealth
RememberScarcity ≠ "expensive" and ≠ "poor countries only". It applies to every society, because wants outrun resources.

Part 1 · Foundations

The 10 principles. Tap to open each.

Three levels: how individuals decide, how agents interact in markets, and how the whole economy behaves.

How individuals make decisions

1People face trade-offs

Every choice gives up something ("you can't have your cake and eat it"). Individuals: time, budget, storage space. Societies: guns or butter, clean environment or higher income, and efficiency vs equality – taxing the rich to help the poor reduces the reward for working hard (a bigger pie or an equal split?).

2The true cost of something is what you give up to get it (opportunity cost)

Opportunity cost = the benefit lost by not choosing the next best alternative. The full cost of studying is not just tuition and books; the biggest part is the income you could have earned. "Free" Facebook is not free: you give up the alternative use of your time.

3Rational agents think at the margin

A marginal change is a small incremental adjustment. A rational agent acts only if marginal benefit > marginal cost. Examples: last-minute travel deals (an empty seat costs almost nothing to fill) and the water–diamond paradox.

4People respond to incentives

Rewards and punishments change behavior. If the price of apples rises, quantity demanded falls and quantity supplied rises. When US gas went from about $2 to $4 (2005–08), people bought smaller cars, scooters, rode transit. Peltzman (1975): seat-belt laws → fewer deaths per accident but more accidents (safer driving → riskier driving).

How agents interact

5Trade can make everyone better off

Specialization lets each person do what they do best and buy the rest. Adam Smith's pin factory (The Wealth of Nations, 1776): ten specialized workers make 48,000+ pins a day; working separately they could hardly make 20 each.

6Markets move toward equilibrium

Equilibrium = nobody can do better by changing their choice. After a shock, individuals' optimizing brings the system to a new equilibrium. Example: supermarket checkout lines even out in wait time; open a new counter and people rearrange until lines are equal again.

7Markets are usually a good way to organize economic activity

The "invisible hand" works through prices: buyers and sellers react to prices, so prices reflect both the value of a good to society and the cost of producing it. Central planners lacked this information, which contributed to the collapse of communism. Price controls and taxes distort the signal.

8Governments can sometimes improve market outcomes

Needed to enforce property rights. Also to fix market failures: an externality (one person's action, such as pollution, affects a bystander) and market power (one seller or a small group sets prices). And possibly to reduce inequality (income tax, welfare), depending on one's political philosophy.

How the economy as a whole works

9One person's spending is another person's income

Chain reactions: US home construction fell >60% between 2005 and 2011 → builders and suppliers lost income → they cut spending → slump spread to the whole economy. This is the circular flow of income.

10A country's standard of living depends on its ability to produce goods and services

Almost all variation in living standards comes from differences in productivity (output per unit of time). Policy to raise living standards: education, capital, best technology, innovation, R&D. (Chart: GDP per capita 2022, about $58k US vs $32k Poland, in 2011 international dollars.)

Is Facebook free? Do the maths.

The lecture prices your time at an opportunity cost. Change the inputs and watch the total.

  1. Time on social media per day (hours) × your hourly opportunity cost = cost per day.
  2. × 365 days = cost per person per year.
  3. × number of adults (254 million in the US in 2019) = cost to the economy.

Lecture's numbers: 1 hour/day × $13 × 365 = $4,745 per adult; × 254 M ≈ $1.2 trillion (about twice Poland's 2019 GDP). The lecture rounds 56 minutes up to 1 hour. If a test asks, use the lecture's figures.

The pointThis does NOT mean social media is a waste. Users get benefits (friends, entertainment). An economist only says the time has a cost; those with large benefits should carry on.

Thinking at the margin: the water–diamond paradox

The puzzle

Water is necessary for survival, diamonds are not. So why is a diamond far more expensive than a cup of water?

The answer

Price follows marginal benefit, which depends on how many units you already have. Water is plentiful → the extra cup adds little. Diamonds are rare → one more adds a lot.

Part 2 · Science and models

Economics as a science.

The essence of science is developing and testing theories about how the world works.

  1. Observe something. Newton saw an apple fall; Friedman noticed people spend differently out of expected vs unexpected income.
  2. Build a theory/hypothesis that generalizes. Newton: gravity acts between any two objects. Friedman (Nobel 1976): the permanent income hypothesis – spending depends more on expected (permanent) income than on transitory (unexpected) income.
  3. Test it against data. Physicists run lab experiments. Economists rarely can, so they use observations (official statistics) and natural experiments from history.

Natural experiment: Oreopoulos (2006)

  1. Before 1947 the UK minimum school-leaving age was 14.
  2. In 1947 it rose to 15, so almost nobody left at 14 any more.
  3. Compare lifetime earnings of people who turned 14 in 1946 vs 1948.
  4. Result: the return to one extra year of education is about 10%.

Why is this clever?

A law changed schooling for one group and not the neighbouring cohort, almost like a randomly assigned treatment. That helps separate causation from mere correlation.

Part 2 · Science and models

Assumptions and models.

"Everything should be made as simple as possible, but not simpler." (attributed to Einstein)

Why assume?

Drop a ball from a 30 m building. Assume free fall (no air friction):

d = ½·g·t² → t = √(2×30 / 9.81) ≈ 2.47 s

We accept a small error to keep the calculation simple. Which clock is better: one that loses a minute a day, or one that doesn't run at all? Economists likewise simplify the world to focus on the most important relationships.

A model is only adequate for its purpose

A flat map is a fine model for finding a route in a city. For a Warsaw → Los Angeles flight (~10,000 km) the Earth's curvature matters, so the shortest route is not a straight line on that map. Each model has a specific application that depends on the assumptions made when building it.

Theory vs model

TheoryModel
Generalized statement explaining a phenomenonApplication of a theory to a specific case
Underlying principlesSpecific phenomenon, narrower focus
Conceptual framework – abstractVerbal or visual representation – practical
QualitativeQuantitative

"Theory without practice is sterile; practice without theory is blind." (Kurt Lewin)

When models are misused

LTCM (1994–98) and the 2007 sub-prime crisisSophisticated finance models (built partly by Nobel laureates) said a run of bad news like 1998 was extremely unlikely. They left out rare, severe scenarios, and the fund collapsed (rescued in part by officials). In 2007 funds omitted a severe downturn in the mortgage market. Lesson: even the best model can miss important possibilities. Judgment still matters.

Part 2 · Science and models

Positive vs normative statements.

Positive: "what is"

Describes the world as it is (scientific approach). Can be confirmed or refuted by evidence. A positive statement can still be false.

Normative: "what ought to be"

Prescribes how the world should be (policy advice). Includes value judgments about good/bad. Typical words: should, ought, fair, unfair, better.

Why do economists disagree?

1. Different positive views

e.g. how strongly saving reacts to tax incentives → different opinions on taxing income vs consumption.

2. Different values (normative)

e.g. what policy should achieve → total taxes paid vs the percentage rate on income.

Chicago Booth's Kent A. Clark Center polls expert panels. Example in the lecture: "Over the past 25 years the income of the median European citizen has declined substantially relative to the median American." Responses (confidence-weighted): strongly agree 17%, agree 33%, uncertain 18%, disagree 29%, strongly disagree 3%. Even experts split on a positive claim, and comments stress medians vs means and Eastern Europe's fast growth.

Part 3 · Model 2

The Island of Despair.

Two people, two goods, scarce resources. This tiny economy teaches opportunity cost, comparative advantage and the PPF.

Robinson Crusoe

Per week: 20 coconuts or 40 fish (or any mix: 20p coconuts and 40(1−p) fish, where p is the share of the week on coconuts).

Friday

Per week: 50 coconuts or 25 fish (mix: 50q coconuts, 25(1−q) fish).

Step 1: opportunity costs

Opportunity cost of a good = (amount of the other good given up) ÷ (amount of this good gained).

RobinsonFriday
Cost of 1 fish20 ÷ 40 = ½ coconut50 ÷ 25 = 2 coconuts
Cost of 1 coconut40 ÷ 20 = 2 fish25 ÷ 50 = ½ fish
Comparative advantageFish (lower cost)Coconuts (lower cost)
Comparative advantage= the lower opportunity cost of producing a good. It is not about who produces more in absolute terms.

Step 2: gains from trade

  1. Specialize by comparative advantage: Robinson catches 40 fish, Friday harvests 50 coconuts.
  2. Trade 20 fish for 20 coconuts.
  3. Robinson: (20 fish, 20 coconuts). Friday: (20 fish, 30 coconuts).
  4. Check: alone, Robinson with 20 coconuts could catch no fish at all, and Friday with 30 coconuts could catch only 10 fish. Neither reaches these bundles alone. Principle 5: trade can make everyone better off.

Part 3 · Interactive

Build the production possibilities frontier.

The PPF shows the combinations of coconuts and fish the island can produce with all its resources and current technology.

How the frontier is built

  1. Start with all fish: Robinson 40 + Friday 25 = 65 fish, 0 coconuts. This is the top of the vertical axis.
  2. Want coconuts? Use the lowest-cost producer first. Friday gives up only ½ fish per coconut. Moving Friday to coconuts gives (50 coconuts, 40 fish). This is the kink.
  3. For more coconuts, Robinson must switch, at 2 fish per coconut. Ends at (70 coconuts, 0 fish).
  4. The slope changes from −½ to −2: rising opportunity cost → a concave (bowed-out) frontier. With many producers the frontier gets smoother.
Shift vs moveA better technology (drag the slider to 50 fish) moves the whole frontier outward; choosing a different point on the frontier is just a different allocation. Principle 10: more production possibilities → more to consume.

Part 4 · Graph guide

How to read a supply and demand graph.

Almost every exam graph is built from the same six ingredients. Learn them once.

  • ①
    Vertical axis = price (P)What a buyer pays or a seller gets per unit (zł, $).
  • ②
    Horizontal axis = quantity (Q)Units per period (per month, per year).
  • ③
    Demand curve DSlopes down: higher price → lower quantity demanded. Each point says "at this price buyers want this quantity". It shows plans, not actual sales.
  • ④
    Supply curve SSlopes up: higher price → higher quantity supplied.
  • ⑤
    Equilibrium EWhere the curves cross: Q demanded = Q supplied. Gives P* and Q*. The market clears.
  • ⑥
    The horizontal gapAt a price above P*, the distance between S and D is the surplus. Below P*, it is the shortage.
Reading trickTo read a graph, pick a price on the vertical axis, go across to the curve, then down to read the quantity. Quantities are always compared horizontally at the same price.

The most-tested distinction: move along vs shift

Movement along a curve

Caused only by a change in the good's own price. Quantity demanded/supplied changes; the curve stays.

Shift of the curve

Caused by anything else (income, other prices, tastes, expectations, number of buyers/sellers, input prices, technology, regulation). The whole curve moves.

Try it: the lab below moves a point along D when you change the price, and shifts D when you change income.

Everything except the good's own price. Right shift = more demanded at every price.

Normal vs inferior

Normal: demand rises with income. Inferior: demand falls as income rises.

Substitutes vs complements

Substitutes: a price fall of one reduces demand for the other. Complements: a price fall of one raises demand for the other (used together).

Case: two ways to reduce smoking

Shift the demand curve

Public announcements, mandatory health warnings, advertising bans: demand shifts left at every price.

Move along the demand curve

Raise price through taxes. Studies: +10% price → −4% quantity demanded (−12% for teenagers).

CarefulTobacco and marijuana: some argue they are substitutes (high cigarette prices push people to marijuana). The empirical evidence in the lecture suggests complements: lower cigarette prices go with greater marijuana use.

Part 5 · Equilibrium

Market equilibrium: the lab.

Equilibrium = the price P* and quantity Q* at which quantity demanded = quantity supplied. At P* the market clears: buyers have bought all they want and sellers have sold all they want (Principle 7).
  1. Pick an event (or shift a curve yourself). Ask first: which curve moves, and which way?
  2. Predict the new P* and Q* before reading the answer.
  3. Turn on "Test a price" to see what happens off-equilibrium: surplus pushes price down, shortage pushes price up, until the market is back at equilibrium (Principle 6).
Numbers used in the labDemand QD = 100 − 5P, supply QS = 5P − 20 → solve 100 − 5P = 5P − 20 → P* = 12, Q* = 40. Shifting demand right by d or supply right by s gives P* = (120 + d − s)/10 and Q* = 40 + (d + s)/2.

Part 5 · Equilibrium

Single and double shifts.

Rule: when one curve shifts, both P and Q are determined. When both shift, one of them is certain and the other is ambiguous. It depends on slopes and the size of the shifts.

Memory trickSame direction (both right or both left) → quantity is certain. Opposite directions → price is certain. Use the event lab in the previous slide: select an event, then press an extra shift button to build a double shift.

Off equilibrium

Price above P*: surplus (excess supply)

QS > QD. Sellers can't sell all they want, so they cut prices. Price falls (a move along both curves) until it reaches P*.

Price below P*: shortage (excess demand)

QD > QS. Too many buyers chase too few goods, so sellers raise prices. Price rises until it reaches P*.

Part 5 · Case study

Why coffee prices spike and then fall back.

Brazil is the world's largest producer. A frost or drought destroys trees → supply falls → price jumps. Then the market adjusts. Step through the four stages.

Facts from the lecture

NY price per pound: 68¢ (1975) → $1.23 (1976) → $2.70 (1977) after the July 1975 frost. Prices jumped again in 1986 after the 1985 drought; 1994–95 about double the 1993 level; and by 2002 a 30-year low of 45¢.

Key idea: elasticity changes over time

Short run: trees are fixed and consumers change habits slowly, so both curves are steep (inelastic) and the price spike is large. Longer run: supply and demand are flatter, so the price comes down; in the long run new trees mature and price returns to its cost-based level.

Part 6 · Price controls

Price floors and price ceilings.

Price floor = legal minimum price

Lobbied by producers who think the market price is too low. If set above P*, quantity supplied > quantity demanded → surplus. Example: minimum wage.

Price ceiling = legal maximum price

Lobbied by consumers who think the price is too high. If set below P*, quantity demanded > quantity supplied → shortage. Example: rent control.

Classic trapA control only matters if it is binding: a floor below P* or a ceiling above P* changes nothing. Try it in the lab.
  1. Find the equilibrium without controls (P* = 12, Q* = 40).
  2. Draw the control price as a horizontal line. Is it on the "binding" side of P*?
  3. If binding, read QD and QS at that price. The gap is the surplus (floor) or shortage (ceiling).
  4. Actual trading = the smaller of the two quantities (the short side of the market).

Part 6 · Price controls

Two real-world cases.

Minimum wage (price floor in the labor market)

Impact depends on the skills of the worker; the biggest effect is on teenage labor (least skilled and experienced). Typical study: +10% minimum wage → −1% to −3% teen employment. It also raises the number of teenagers who look for jobs (some drop out of school), who displace others who become unemployed.

For

  • Raises income of the working poor
  • Counter-measure to the market power of large employers

Against

  • Unemployment among unskilled workers
  • Discourages training; teenagers dropping out
  • Poorly targeted: fewer than ⅓ of minimum-wage earners live in poor families

Rent control (price ceiling in housing)

  1. Short run: supply of apartments and demand are inelastic, so effects are not obvious at first.
  2. Long run: landlords build fewer new apartments and maintain existing ones less; low rents draw more people to the city and discourage sharing → large shortage and falling quality. ("The best way to destroy a city, other than bombing.")
  3. Rationing: waiting lists and interviews, discrimination (children, social class, nationality, race), under-the-table payments to managers (which push the total price toward the equilibrium price).
Exam angleFor any control: say which curve the price line cuts, whether it is binding, the direction and size of the gap, and then the side effects (rationing, quality, unemployment).

Part 6 · Taxes

Who really pays the tax?

Tax incidence = how the burden of a tax is distributed between buyers and sellers.
  1. Gross price (demand price) PD = what the buyer pays. Net price (supply price) PS = what the seller keeps after the tax is passed on. So PD − PS = t.
  2. Tax on sellers: shift the (inverse) supply curve up by t. Sellers need PS + t to supply a given quantity.
  3. Tax on buyers: shift the (inverse) demand curve down by t. Buyers will only pay PD − t to the seller.
  4. The new equilibrium quantity Q′ is smaller. Both versions give the same Q′, PD and PS: the burden doesn't depend on who formally pays.
Key takeaways① Buyers pay more (PD − P*), sellers get less (P* − PS). ② Government revenue = t × Q′ (the shaded rectangle). ③ Quantity falls, so taxes distort decisions (Principle 7). ④ The burden falls more on the less responsive (steeper) side. That is a preview of elasticity (next lecture).

Review

One-page cheat sheet.

Core definitions

  • Scarcity: limited resources vs unlimited wants
  • Opportunity cost: next best alternative given up
  • Marginal thinking: act if MB > MC
  • Comparative advantage: lower opportunity cost
  • Equilibrium: QD = QS; no one gains by changing
  • Market failure: externality, market power
  • Positive: testable "is" · Normative: value-based "should"

Formulas & rules

  • Opp. cost = (give up) ÷ (gain)
  • Market demand/supply = sum of quantities at each price
  • Surplus = QS − QD (at price above P*)
  • Shortage = QD − QS (at price below P*)
  • Tax: PD − PS = t; revenue = t × Q′
  • PPF slope = opportunity cost; concave ⇒ rising opportunity cost

Shifts at a glance

Demand shifts: income (normal ↑/inferior ↓), substitutes, complements, tastes, expectations, number of buyers.
Supply shifts: input prices, technology, regulation, expectations, number of sellers.
Own price → movement along the curve only.

Traps the test loves

  • Shift vs movement along the curve
  • Non-binding floor/ceiling has no effect
  • Double shift: only one of P, Q is certain
  • Positive ≠ true
  • Substitutes vs complements direction
  • Add curves horizontally (quantities), not vertically
